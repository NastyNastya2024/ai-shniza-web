4 FOCAL LENGTHS
FOR YOUR AI VIDEOS

24mm / 70mm / 135mm / 200mm

Learn how each focal length changes the frame - and how to tell an AI video model exactly what should stay visible.


1. What the millimeters mean
24mm, 70mm, 135mm and 200mm are focal lengths - numbers that describe a lens's field of view.

If the camera and subject stay in the same positions and the aspect ratio does not change:

A shorter focal length shows more of the environment.
A longer focal length creates a tighter frame and makes the subject appear larger.
Focal length alone does not determine shot size. You can film either a face or a full-body shot at 135mm; the result also depends on how far the camera is from the subject.

That is why an AI prompt should specify both the focal length and exactly what must appear inside the frame.

02 / CHOOSE YOUR FOCAL LENGTH

Match the lens to the shot
Use the focal length as a visual cue, then define the framing in plain language. These are practical starting points, not strict rules.


FOCAL LENGTH

BEST USED WHEN

DESCRIBE THE FRAME AS

All focal lengths in this guide are full-frame equivalents, which gives you one consistent reference for comparing fields of view.

03 / WRITE THE PROMPT

Describe the frame, not just the lens
A useful prompt follows this order:

Framing → focal length → what must stay visible → action → camera movement

Writing only 135mm lens is not enough. Add clear frame boundaries and behavior:

Close-up with a 135mm full-frame-equivalent lens look.
Frame the subject from the shoulders up, keeping the entire
head visible. The subject looks toward the camera with
a subtle smile. Restrained handheld movement. No zoom.

This gives the model a complete visual task: a close-up, defined frame boundaries, one simple action and restrained handheld movement.

In AI generation, focal length is a visual cue, not a guarantee that the model will reproduce the exact optics of a physical lens. Judge the result by the image: how large the subject appears, how much of the environment remains visible and whether the intended reaction reads clearly.

04 / READY-TO-USE PROMPT BLOCKS

24mm and 70mm
Choose one block and add it to your scene description. Define the character, location, lighting, aspect ratio and duration separately.

If you are using a reference image as the first frame, prepare the correct framing in advance. That source image already establishes the composition.

24MM - SUBJECT AND ENVIRONMENT
Use this when the location is part of the story.

Wide shot with a 24mm full-frame-equivalent lens look.
Show the subject from head to toe, with generous space around
them and a clearly readable environment. Keep the subject
away from the extreme frame edges.

The subject takes one relaxed step and looks across the
location. Restrained handheld movement from a fixed camera
position. No zoom or camera travel.

70MM - SUBJECT, WARDROBE AND GESTURES
Use this when the viewer needs to read both the expression and the hands.

Medium shot with a 70mm full-frame-equivalent lens look.
Frame the subject from the waist up, keeping the entire head
and both hands visible. Show a limited portion of the environment.

The subject briefly adjusts their sleeve, then looks toward
the camera. Restrained handheld movement from a fixed camera
position. No zoom or camera travel.

PROMPT BLOCKS CONTINUED

135mm and 200mm
135MM - FACE AND REACTION
Use this when a look, smile or small facial change carries the meaning.

Close-up with a 135mm full-frame-equivalent lens look.
Frame the subject from the shoulders up, keeping the entire
head visible. Make the eyes and facial expression clearly readable.

The subject turns slightly toward the camera and gives
a restrained, knowing smile. Subtle breathing and natural
blinking. Restrained handheld movement from a fixed camera
position. No zoom or camera travel.

200MM - TIGHT FRAMING FROM FAR AWAY
Use this when the camera observes from across a courtyard, street or large interior.

Close-up from a distant camera position, with a 200mm
full-frame-equivalent telephoto lens look. The subject's
head and shoulders fill most of the frame.

The subject pauses their work and looks toward the camera.
Keep the eyes sharp and the expression natural. Restrained
handheld movement from the same distant position.
No zoom or forward camera travel.

05 / RECREATE THE REEL COMPARISON

Change only the focal length
To compare 24mm, 70mm and 135mm fairly, keep the following elements unchanged:

the same subject and location;
the camera and subjective positions;
the camera height and viewing direction;
the lighting and aspect ratio.
Then change only the focal length. Add this block to the prompt:

Keep the camera position, camera height, viewing direction
and subject position identical across all three shots.
Use the same sensor format and aspect ratio.

Change only the focal length: 24mm, 70mm, then 135mm.
Allow the framing to become progressively tighter.
Do not move the camera or subject to compensate.

Do not ask the model to keep the subject the same size. In this comparison, the subject should become progressively larger in the frame.

For greater control, prepare three matched reference images first, check the compositions, then animate each one separately. Keep the subject's movement small so it does not distract from the focal-length comparison.

06 / COMMON MIX-UPS

Know what actually changes the shot
Focal length vs. camera-to-subject distance
Both affect how large the subject appears. Moving the camera also changes perspective - the spatial relationship between near and distant objects. Changing focal length from one fixed camera position does not change perspective by itself.

Close-up vs. blurred background
These are separate choices. If you want a softer background, request it directly:

Softly blurred background, eyes in sharp focus.

Focal length vs. camera movement
200mm is a focal length.

A crash zoom is a rapid change in focal length during the shot.

A dolly in is the camera physically moving toward the subject.

If you want the shot to begin tight, write Start on a close-up. Add camera movement only when you want the audience to see the approach itself.

Try it with your own character
Create two shots: 24mm to establish the environment, then 135mm to reveal the reaction.

In each prompt, specify exactly what must stay visible. This lets you guide the viewer's attention: first show the place, then isolate what matters.
